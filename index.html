BEFORE WE START

Four questions. This is not a test.

There is a difference between finding out where something is shaky and finding out what is causing it. Four questions can only do the first job.

So if a question does not go well, we will not decide you are bad at the topic. We will ask one or two more, narrower questions until we can name the exact step that slipped.

You will also be asked how sure you felt. Answer that honestly. A lucky guess and a confident answer look identical on paper, and only one of them means you are ready.

There is an I am not sure option every time. Choosing it costs you nothing and helps you a lot.

STEP 3 | NAMING THE PARTS

Every piece of that equation has a job.

C(x) = 700 + 400x
700The base fare. What you owe before the car has moved a single metre.
400The rate. How much the fare changes for each extra kilometre.
xThe distance. The quantity that varies, and the one you feed in.
C(x)The fare that comes out. It depends on x, which is what the notation says.

One line now holds every row of the table and every point on the graph. That compression is what the notation buys you, and it is why the rest of Calculus is written this way.

WHAT YOU CAN NOW DO

You can describe a relationship. Now watch it break.

A fare adds the same ₦400 every kilometre. A water cart adds the same ₦250 every drum. Steady, predictable, straight lines, and every one of them held a single rate that never wavered.

But some quantities refuse to add. They multiply instead. They double, or they halve, over and over, and writing that out the long way falls apart almost immediately.

You are going to need a more compact kind of notation.

SCALING

Not everything grows by a fixed amount.

A fare adds the same ₦400 every kilometre. A water cart adds the same ₦250 every drum. Steady, predictable, straight.

But some quantities do not add at all. They multiply. They double, or they halve, over and over, and the moment that happens your straight-line tools stop being enough.

STEP 2 | RELIEF

Nobody is writing that out ever again.

Ten halves in a row was already painful. Now imagine a hundred passes. The long form does not just look untidy, it becomes genuinely unusable.

So instead of writing every half, we count them:

64 × (½)10
The ½what happens each pass
The raised 10how many times it happens

That raised number is doing one job and one job only. It counts repetitions. That is all an index has ever been. Written generally, after n passes the file is 64 × (½)n.

STEP 3 | THE MINUS SIGN

Halving is dividing. Dividing has its own notation.

Halving three times means dividing by 2 three times, which means dividing by 8. Three ways to write one idea:

(½)3  =  1 ÷ 23  =  2-3

So which of these equals x-2?

WHAT YOU CAN NOW WRITE

You have the notation. Now go and measure something.

A minus sign in the index flips a term into a fraction. A fraction in the index takes a root. Neither of those is a new rule to memorise, they are the same counting idea stretched to cover division and roots.

You can now describe a relationship, and you can handle the symbols inside it however awkward they look.

What you still cannot do is say how fast any of it is changing. That is next.

WATER

You can describe a relationship and handle the symbols inside it. This is the part Phase 0 was pointing at all along. Not just that a quantity changes, but exactly how fast, in a number you can calculate, defend and act on.

STEP 3 | THE MEASURE

That calculation has a name.

Whatever the two quantities are, you did the same thing. You found how much the output changed, found how much the input changed, and divided one by the other.

change in output  ÷  change in input
For the tank30 L ÷ 5 min = 6 L/min

The units are not decoration. Six on its own means nothing at all. Six litres per minute is something you can act on: it tells you the tank gains six litres in every single minute the pump runs.

STEP 4 | MEASURE AND MEANING

The same number, three different meanings.

Each of these has a gradient of exactly 6. The mathematics that produced them is identical. What they tell you is not.

6
litres per minute
A tank gains six litres in every minute.
6
naira per item
Each extra item adds six naira to the bill.
6
kilometres per hour
Distance grows by six kilometres each hour.

Gradient is the mathematical measure. Rate of change is what that measure means in the situation you are looking at. You calculate the gradient. You interpret the rate.

STEP 5 | RUNNING BACKWARDS

The same calculation, the other direction.

Later that day the tank is being drained for cleaning. At 2:00pm it holds 140 litres. At 2:20pm it holds 60 litres.

Rate = L/min

A negative rate is not an error and not a smaller number. It is the same measurement pointing the other way. The size tells you how fast. The sign tells you which direction.

WHAT YOU FOUND

Can we keep making the interval smaller?

A wide interval gave you an average that the tank never actually achieved at any single moment. As you narrowed it, the number stopped wandering and closed in on how the tank was behaving right there, at minute eight.

You still needed two points to do the calculation. You always have. But the gap between them got very small indeed, and the answer kept making sense.

Hold that thought. Environment 6 takes it to its conclusion.

BUSINESS

Now put the whole toolkit to work.

You can build a function, handle the algebra inside it, and measure how fast it changes. That is enough to answer a question that costs people real money.

Two printers want your business. Only one of them is the right choice, and which one it is depends entirely on a number you have not decided yet.

STEP 1 | TWO QUOTES

Two printers, two ways of charging.

PRINTER A
Charges ₦5,000 to set up the printing plates, then ₦20 for every flyer you order.
A(n) = 5,000 + 20n
PRINTER B
No setup fee at all. You simply pay ₦45 for every flyer you order.
B(n) = 45n

B looks cheaper. There is nothing to pay before you start, and A wants five thousand naira before printing a single flyer. But look at the two rates. B charges more than twice as much per flyer.

STEP 2 | DECIDE

How many flyers do you actually need?

YOUR ORDER

Printer A₦5,000
Printer B₦0
At 0 flyers, B costs you nothing. A has already charged ₦5,000.
Move the slider across the full range. Watch which line sits lower.
STEP 3 | PIN IT DOWN

Find the exact crossing point.

You saw the lines cross. The graph told you roughly where. Algebra will tell you exactly. At the crossing, the two costs are equal:

5,000 + 20n = 45n
n = flyers
STEP 4 | SAY IT PROPERLY

Now write the decision as a rule.

A single number is not advice. The shop needs a rule it can apply to any order it places in future. You need to say not just where the lines cross, but which side of the crossing means what.

You need 500 flyers. Which statement is both correct and useful?

WHAT DECIDED IT

Notice what actually settled the argument.

Not the setup fee, and not the price of one flyer. What decided it was the rate. B charges ₦45 per flyer against A's ₦20, and that steeper rate eventually overwhelms any head start A gave away.

Both of these were straight lines, so each one had a single rate that never changed. That made them easy to compare. One crossing, one clean answer.

Very little in the real world is that obliging. Next you meet a line that refuses to hold one rate at all.

THE DOORWAY

So what happens when the two points become the same point?

You never actually put B on top of A. You could not. The moment the gap in x reaches zero the division stops meaning anything at all.

And yet the number was clearly going somewhere, and you could read it off the screen without ever arriving.

Sit with that. It is exactly the question the whole of Calculus was built to answer, and it is where Phase 2 begins.
